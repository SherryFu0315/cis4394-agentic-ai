You Built Agents. Now What?

Mapping your skills to real jobs and career opportunities.

What job is this course for? Here is my answer, using the data available in November 2026. I lined up what you learned this semester against six public sources.

  • Three from OpenAI: a classification of 921 occupations ↗, a benchmark built from real work in 44 occupations ↗, and statistics on what people ask ChatGPT to do at work ↗.
  • Three from elsewhere: a 2023 study of which tasks AI can speed up ↗, the BLS ten-year projections ↗, and a count of job postings by Lightcast ↗.
TL;DR
  • The skills point to growing jobs. Of the 34 jobs that use what you learned, 25 are in the group OpenAI expects to grow with AI, and 25 hire straight out of a bachelor’s.
  • Good first jobs for an MIS graduate: computer systems analyst, data scientist or BI analyst, software developer, software QA tester and project management specialist. Less obvious ones: logistics analyst, web and digital interface designer and validation engineer.
  • Under pressure: help desk, network and systems administration, computer programming and customer service.
  • The map is checked. Against the official O*NET task lists, the jobs I picked rank around the 91st percentile for their skills among 330 knowledge-work jobs, and the check added six jobs I had missed (Part 4).
  • Questions? Ask at the bottom of the page. If data can answer it, I will add the analysis here.

Part 2 · Where an MIS degree leads now

A SWOT for an MIS graduate in 2026

Here is how I would size up the market if I were graduating with an MIS degree and this course behind me. Strengths and weaknesses are about you. Opportunities and threats come from the data further down. Click any job to find it on the chart.

Strengths · what this course gave you

  • You can build a working agent with tools, memory and LangGraph (Weeks 4 to 7, 9).
  • You can show whether it works, with pass^k, traces and a blind comparison (Weeks 8 and 11).
  • You can say what could go wrong and put an approval step in front of it (Week 10).
  • You can argue whether a task needs an agent at all, and what each run costs (Weeks 1 and 11).

Weaknesses · what this course did not cover

  • Training models and the math behind them. PATH’s Deep Learning course covers that.
  • Running software at scale: cloud infrastructure, monitoring and on-call. You shipped a demo, not a production service.
  • Credentials some jobs screen for, such as a CPA or a security certification.
  • Years of experience. Manager roles on this page ask for five or more.

Part 4 · Checking the map

Does this map hold up? I tested it against O*NET

I matched skill groups to jobs by hand, so I checked the result against an independent source: the official task lists the Department of Labor keeps for 923 occupations in O*NET ↗. A text-similarity model compared what each skill group covers, using the course pages and the skills inventory, with the tasks each job actually involves. Where the check disagreed with me, I changed the map.

What I changed

  • Merged two groups. “Build the agent” and “Work with it every day” matched the same jobs, so they are now one group, “Build and use agents”.
  • Removed three jobs. Human resources specialists, training and development specialists, and accountants and auditors. People in these jobs use AI tools, but their task lists do not involve the skills this course teaches.
  • Flagged one job. Market research and marketing specialists stay in, marked as a weak fit.
  • Added six jobs: web and digital interface designers, database architects, clinical data managers, validation engineers, security management specialists and logistics analysts.

How the check works, and what it cannot tell you
  • Jobs: 19,265 O*NET task statements for 923 occupations, with core tasks counted twice.
  • Skill groups: the text of the course pages that teach each group, plus the skill descriptions from the course skills inventory.
  • Matching: each task and each piece of course text is turned into a vector with a small open model (nomic-embed-text). A job’s score on a group is the average similarity of its five best-matching tasks, compared across all 923 jobs.
  • Significance: the share of correct matches is compared with 10,000 random reshuffles of the groups.
  • Limits: O*NET task lists are general and change slowly, so the check picks up shared ground like security, testing and analysis better than anything specific to AI agents. It uses one model, and no person has yet coded the jobs independently. Group 5 is not tested this way, because those jobs are listed for the work agents take over, not for the skills they use.

Most people use ChatGPT at work as a writing assistant. The things an agent does, like writing code, fixing computer problems and looking things up through tools, add up to less than 8% of work messages. Agents that do this work for other people are still built by a fairly small group of developers. This course trains you to join them.

Less of it is about work

In July 2024, 51.6% of consumer messages worldwide were work-related. By September 2026 the figure was 32.2%, and 33.1% in the U.S. Use outside work grew faster. Business accounts are left out of this data, so it can’t tell you how much AI is used at work overall.

At work, people hand off tasks

In September 2026, 47.8% of work messages asked the model to produce something. Another 27.3% asked it for information. Outside work it goes the other way, with 41.5% asking and 27.3% producing. Handing off a task is exactly what an agent is for.

Georgia is seventh

Georgia ranked 10th among U.S. states for ChatGPT messages per person in early 2025. It has been 7th for each of the last three quarters. Of all the topics, technical help is the most tied to work. 57% of those messages are work-related, compared with 49% for writing.

Where the numbers come from · 2 · OpenAI AI Jobs Transition Framework

Four paths for 921 occupations

OpenAI published this framework ↗ on April 25, 2026, with a full report ↗ and a downloadable occupation table. Most studies of AI and jobs ask one question, whether AI can do the tasks in a job. This one asks two more. Does a person still need to deliver the work or take responsibility for it? And if AI makes the service cheaper, will people buy enough more of it to keep the workers busy? Each occupation lands in one of four groups based on the answers. OpenAI is careful to say the groups are not forecasts of job loss. They show where change is likely to appear first.

U.S. employment by archetype · 2024 · from the public occupation table (923 rows, 152.1 million jobs)
  • Jobs with less immediate change449 occupations · mostly physical, place-bound work
    71.9M47%
  • Jobs that will reorganize232 occupations · a person still has to sign off or be present
    35.3M23%
  • Jobs at higher automation risk91 occupations · standardized information work, no human-necessity flag
    26.9M18%
  • Jobs that grow with AI151 occupations · demand elasticity near or above 1
    18.0M12%

The number that separates the groups is demand elasticity. Above 1 means that when a service gets 10% cheaper, people buy more than 10% more of it. The occupation can then add workers even as each worker gets more done. The highest value in OpenAI’s table, 1.15, belongs to computer systems analysts and computer network architects. Software developers are at 1.13 and data scientists at 1.12. Customer service representatives are at 0.68, and general office clerks at 0.41.

One caution before you read further. OpenAI’s write-up ↗ names software developers and financial analysts as examples of jobs that will reorganize. Its downloadable table puts both in the grow-with-AI group. I used the table everywhere on this page.

Two official sources, two different answers. OpenAI’s table puts information security analysts in the higher-automation-risk group. BLS projects ↗ the same occupation to grow 28.5% by 2034, one of the fastest rates among computer jobs. They are measuring different things. OpenAI asks how much of today’s task list a model could do. BLS asks how many people employers plan to hire. My reading is that the routine part of security work is exposed, and the judgment about what an agent is allowed to touch is what employers are hiring for. That judgment is Week 10.

Customer service shows the same gap from the other side. About 70% of a representative’s tasks are exposed, and BLS projects 153,700 fewer of these jobs by 2034. The people who build and supervise the agents that take over that work are counted in other occupations, mostly the ones in groups 1 to 4 above.

Where the numbers come from · 3 · GDPval

44 occupations whose real work is now a benchmark

For GDPval ↗, OpenAI picked the nine sectors that contribute most to U.S. GDP. In each one it took the five best-paid occupations that are mostly done on a computer. Professionals from those jobs, with 14 years of experience on average, wrote 1,320 tasks from their own work. Each task comes with reference files and a finished product, such as a spreadsheet, a legal brief or a care plan. Other experts then compare a model’s product with the human one without knowing which is which, and score it as a win, a tie or a loss. The 220 public tasks are on Hugging Face ↗. What the score means for you

In the September 2025 paper ↗, the best model matched or beat the expert on 47.6% of tasks. The runner-up scored 38.8%, and a 2024 model 12.4%. The live leaderboard ↗ has moved since then.

Look at what gets judged. It is the finished file, and the conversation that produced it doesn’t count. Then look at how it gets judged: an expert compares two outputs side by side with the labels hidden. You did the same thing with the harness in Week 8 and the rubric in Group Assignment 3. I expect employers in these occupations to need people who can run that kind of test on their own work.

What it leaves out

Every GDPval task arrives with a clear prompt and all the files the model needs. The model never has to work out what the task is, ask a clarifying question, or recover when a tool breaks. That is most of what an agent does, and most of what we covered in Weeks 4 to 10. A good GDPval score tells you the writing and analysis hold up. The benchmark doesn’t test the loop around them.

Where the numbers come from · 4 · Job postings

What employers are posting

Old job titles, new skills

Lightcast ↗ counted more than 86,000 job postings in 2025 that asked for agentic AI skills, up more than 280% in a year. The median advertised salary was $153,000. For AI postings in general it was $141,000, and for IT $125,000.

Fortune 1000 companies posted about 30% of these jobs, although they post only 12% of jobs overall. Amazon, Microsoft, KPMG and Accenture are among the top ten employers. Six of the ten occupations that ask most often are technical, including AI engineers and software developers. The authors point out that a search by job title would barely find this work, because companies are adding it to jobs that already exist.

Where to look, and what to write

  • Titles to search: AI engineer, agent engineer, applied AI engineer, forward-deployed engineer, solutions engineer (AI), AI product manager, AI evaluation or quality engineer, AI governance or risk analyst, automation analyst, systems analyst.
  • Keywords résumé filters look for: LangGraph, LangChain, LangSmith, Model Context Protocol, tool calling, function calling, RAG, evaluation harness, pass^k, LLM-as-judge, prompt injection, guardrails, human-in-the-loop, OWASP Agentic Top 10, NIST AI RMF.
  • One sentence about your capstone, with numbers in it. Something like “Built a LangGraph purchasing agent with four tools and an approval step. pass^5 = 0.72 on 40 traced tasks. Blocked 9 of 9 prompt-injection attempts.” Put in your real numbers. An interviewer can ask about every part of it, and you will have an answer.
  • Lightcast’s other number. In early 2026, jobs with AI in the title were 0.3% of all postings and 7% of AI adoption. The other 93% came from ordinary jobs that now list AI skills. Most of the jobs on this page fall in that 93%.
Read with care

What this data can and can’t tell you

Usage statistics

Signals ↗

These are shares of consumer messages, with noise added for privacy. They show what people ask ChatGPT to do. They don’t show whether anyone used the answer, and they leave out business and API traffic, which is where most agents run.

Exposure and classification

GPTs are GPTs ↗ · the framework ↗

Both start from the O*NET list of tasks for each job. The exposure scores describe what a 2023 model could do. The 2026 framework adds whether a person is still needed and how demand responds. Neither one watches jobs actually change.

Task performance

GDPval ↗

Experts graded finished work on clearly defined tasks. OpenAI built the benchmark and grades its own models on it, although the leaderboard lists other companies’ models too. It measures the quality of the output. It leaves out the rest of a workday, like figuring out what the task is or recovering from a mistake.
